Executive Summary

Decision-oriented overview

This project examines demographic, personal, and workplace factors associated with mental health treatment-seeking behavior in the technology workplace. The analysis combines data cleaning, feature engineering, exploratory analysis, multivariate analysis, Spearman correlation, and Chi-square testing with Cramér's V effect sizes.

The strongest association is work interference, followed by family history, care options, mental health benefits, and gender. The analysis distinguishes statistically supported relationships from non-significant factors and translates the results into practical workplace recommendations.

Key Analytical Signals

What matters most for decision-making

💼 Work interference is the strongest signal

Treatment rates increase from 14.1% for respondents reporting Never to 70.5% Rarely, 77.0% Sometimes, and 85.4% Often. The approximately 71 percentage-point gap is supported by a strong Cramér's V of 0.542.

👥 Family history shows a substantial association

Treatment was reported by 74.2% of respondents with a family history compared with 35.5% without one. Cramér's V is approximately 0.375.

🏥 Access and awareness matter

Care options and mental health benefits both show statistically significant associations with treatment behavior, highlighting the importance of making workplace support visible and accessible.

📊 Not every observed difference is statistically meaningful

Age group, remote work status, company size, supervisor support, and selected physical-health perception variables did not show statistically significant associations with treatment.

Geographic Analysis

Sample representation, not global prevalence

The project includes worldwide respondent distribution and a United States state-level view. These visuals establish where the survey sample is concentrated and provide geographic context. A comprehensive country/state-by-attitude comparison was not claimed because the sample is strongly concentrated in the United States. Geographic results should therefore be interpreted as sample representation rather than global prevalence.

Business Recommendations

Population-level workplace actions
🎯 Prioritize early support

Use work interference as a population-level signal for strengthening early support and referral pathways.

🏥 Improve care visibility

Clearly communicate available mental health care options so employees know where and how to seek support.

🛟 Strengthen benefit communication

Make mental health benefit coverage and access pathways easy to understand.

🔐 Reinforce confidentiality

Make privacy and anonymity policies clear and visible to employees.

🤝 Build supportive culture

Reduce stigma around disclosure and strengthen the workplace environment surrounding mental health.

⚖️ Avoid demographic profiling

Use analytics to improve population-level support systems rather than labeling or profiling individuals.

Limitations & Responsible Interpretation

Important context for stakeholders
  • The dataset was collected in 2014 and may not represent current workplace conditions.
  • The survey is self-reported and observational.
  • The sample has strong U.S. representation and is not globally representative.
  • Treatment rate is an outcome measure and should not be interpreted as mental-health prevalence.
  • Statistical relationships represent associations rather than causal effects.
  • The comments field contains substantial missing data and was excluded from quantitative analysis.
  • Small geographic groups should not be used for strong comparative conclusions.
